# What Gas Hydrates Are and Why They Form in Subsea Lines

Gas hydrates (clathrate hydrates) are ice-like crystalline solids in which gas molecules — typically methane, ethane, propane, or CO2 — become trapped inside cages of hydrogen-bonded water molecules. They are non-stoichiometric inclusion compounds that can form at temperatures well above the normal freezing point of water, provided the pressure is high enough.

## Formation conditions

Hydrate formation requires three simultaneous conditions:

1. **High pressure** — typically above 10-30 bar, as found in deepwater flowlines.
2. **Low temperature** — typically below 20-25 C at relevant pressures.
3. **Free water** — liquid water must be present in contact with the gas phase.

Subsea oil and gas production lines are uniquely susceptible because they operate at high wellhead pressures (often 200-350 bar) and sit on the cold seabed (2-4 C in deep water). As produced fluids travel from the warm reservoir through the cold subsea environment, they cross into the hydrate stability region.

## Why hydrates are dangerous

Once nucleated, hydrate crystals can grow rapidly, agglomerating into plugs that block flowlines. A full hydrate plug can:

- Stop production entirely (sometimes for weeks).
- Create a pressure differential across the plug that, if released suddenly, can propel the plug like a projectile — a severe safety hazard.
- Damage subsea equipment, requiring costly intervention.

The transition from first crystal to full blockage can take hours to days, which is why early detection of hydrate-forming conditions is critical.
