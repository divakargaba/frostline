# Thermodynamic Inhibitors: Methanol vs MEG

Thermodynamic hydrate inhibitors (THIs) work by shifting the hydrate equilibrium curve to lower temperatures and higher pressures, effectively widening the safe operating window. The two most common THIs are methanol and monoethylene glycol (MEG).

## Methanol

- **Mechanism:** Shifts hydrate equilibrium by lowering water activity. Very effective per unit mass.
- **Typical dose:** 10-50 wt% in the water phase, depending on required temperature depression.
- **Advantages:** Low cost per unit of inhibition, effective at high subcooling, fast-acting.
- **Disadvantages:** Volatile — lost to the gas phase (up to 30-50% in some systems), toxic, difficult to recover and recycle, flammable.
- **Use case:** Often preferred for intermittent use (shutdowns, restarts) or when recovery is impractical.

## Monoethylene glycol (MEG)

- **Mechanism:** Same thermodynamic principle as methanol, but heavier molecular weight requires higher concentrations.
- **Typical dose:** 30-60 wt% in the water phase.
- **Advantages:** Non-volatile (stays in the liquid phase), recoverable and recyclable via MEG regeneration units, lower toxicity than methanol.
- **Disadvantages:** Higher initial cost per unit of inhibition, requires regeneration infrastructure, higher viscosity at low temperatures.
- **Use case:** Preferred for continuous injection in deepwater systems where a MEG loop can be built.

## Choosing between them

The choice depends on the field architecture:
- **Methanol** for short-duration needs, well interventions, and systems without recovery infrastructure.
- **MEG** for continuous deepwater production with dedicated regeneration facilities.

Both are dosed using the Hammerschmidt equation or more sophisticated thermodynamic models to determine the required concentration for a given temperature shift.
