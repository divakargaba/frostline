# Hydrate Equilibrium Curve and Subcooling

## The equilibrium curve

The hydrate equilibrium curve (also called the hydrate dissociation curve) plots the pressure-temperature boundary where hydrates are thermodynamically stable. Above and to the left of this curve (higher pressure, lower temperature), hydrates can form. Below and to the right, they dissociate.

The curve depends on gas composition and water salinity. For a typical natural gas (specific gravity ~0.65), the equilibrium temperature at 250 bar is roughly 20-22 C. Heavier gases shift the curve to higher temperatures, making hydrates more likely.

## Subcooling and its significance

**Subcooling** (or undercooling) is the difference between the actual fluid temperature and the hydrate equilibrium temperature at the current pressure:

    subcooling = T_equilibrium(P) - T_actual

A positive subcooling means the system is inside the hydrate stability region. The larger the subcooling, the greater the thermodynamic driving force for hydrate formation and the faster crystals nucleate and grow.

- **Subcooling < 0 C (negative margin):** Outside hydrate region, safe.
- **Subcooling 0-3 C:** Approaching the boundary, caution needed.
- **Subcooling > 3 C:** Strong driving force, rapid formation expected.

## Operational use

Operators monitor the subcooling margin (or equivalently, the hydrate margin) as a primary early warning indicator. A shrinking margin — even before it reaches zero — signals that conditions are trending toward hydrate formation and warrants investigation.
